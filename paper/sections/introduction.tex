% ============================================================
% Introduction
% ============================================================

A growing body of theory predicts that \textit{anticipated} third-party
intervention shapes civil war onset
\citep{cetinyan2002,cunningham2016,dudley2024,gibilisco2022,kuperman2008,kyddstraus2013,lango2023,sambanis2020},
yet we lack the measure it calls for: one that covers the full population of
potential interveners, varies annually with shifting alliances and
foreign-policy alignments, and treats direction---government- versus
opposition-biased---as a quantity in its own right.  This paper constructs it,
and uses it to show that the decision to fight turns on the direction of the
anticipated intervention environment and is checked by its intensity.

The construction is the core contribution.  A machine-learning ensemble
trained on the \citet{regan2000} dataset of military
interventions (1946--2014) predicts, for each directed dyad in a given
year, the probability and direction of intervention.  Dyad-level
predictions are aggregated across all potential interveners to produce a
country-year shadow, disciplined by a Nash fixed-point condition
requiring each state's predicted probability to be self-consistent as an
input to the others' predictions.

The measure is built at the level of intervention decisions---who is expected
to intervene, and on whose side.  Whether it bears on onset is a separate
question, and the way to ask it is to carry the shadow into a standard
country-year onset logit \citep{fearon2003}.  It earns its place there: added
to the structural baseline, the shadow improves out-of-sample prediction---
modestly in aggregate, and substantially once resolved by intervener type---and
it encompasses the leading existing measures, each capturing only one side of
intervention or the major powers.  Only once it predicts is there reason to
read its direction.  The bargaining literature offers three common accounts of
why a war both sides could avoid happens anyway: the government's strength or
resolve is private, and the opposition's demand screens it; the government
cannot credibly keep tomorrow the concession it makes today; or the two sides
read the odds of intervention itself differently.  Worked through a single
framework, the three stake different predictions on the shadow's two
axes---the tilt registering which way a war would lean, the intensity the
burden it would impose on those who fight it.  The estimates lean among these
accounts rather than decide between them: onset falls with the intensity in
every measurement draw and rises with the tilt in a clear majority, a pattern
the standard screening model does not produce and the other two do.  The
evidence points away from the screen and toward limited commitment or divergent
expectations, without separating those two.

The pieces of this account are not new; what has been missing is holding them
together.  \citet{cunningham2016} established the government side---the
prospect of great-power backing for an incumbent can deter a rebellion
outright, shifting the expected balance so far that no challenge pays.
\citet{lango2023}, whose model of civil war fought in the shadow of
intervention is the most similar to the one developed here, established the
opposition side---rebels weigh anticipated outside support, and its prospect
emboldens the challenge, even as, pushed far enough, it can induce a government
to concede rather than fight.  \citet{gibilisco2022} brought structural
estimation to the question, recovering intervention expectations from an
equilibrium among the major powers and finding their effects to run in opposite
directions for different powers---a heterogeneity that foreshadows one of the
results below.  Each holds a piece: one side of intervention, or one set of
sponsors---but the ones left out are not a fringe.  In these data, fewer than
half of the interventions---\PFivePct\ percent---are the work of the major
powers; the rest fall to neighbors, coethnics, rivals, and Cold War proxies.
None of these accounts holds both sides together across that full population,
and the gap is not incidental.  Because a country that draws government-biased
intervention tends to draw the opposition-biased kind as well, the net tilt of
the anticipated environment comes into view only when both sides are measured
at once, across everyone, and over time.  The field has
seen the two forces one at a time; seeing their difference has required
measuring them together.

Measuring that environment is the hard part, and it fixes the method.  The
quantity is an expectation---for every potential intervener, on each side, in
each year, the probability that it takes a hand---and no general theory of
intervention tells us which states may be dropped from the roster, or which
side left out of the ledger.  What remains is a large and noisy classification problem, and the
task at the measurement stage is to get the probabilities right.  This is the natural home of flexible, predictive
machinery, and it is the opposite of the choice a structural analysis makes:
rather than pare the problem to a set small enough to solve exactly, the
learned-proxy approach keeps the whole of it and brings the strongest available
predictor to bear, holding interpretation for the second stage
\citep{knoxlucascho2022, moruccispirling2024}.  The structural route is not
discarded but embedded---the softmax best response of \citet{gibilisco2022}
enters the ensemble library as one candidate among many, and the data award it
\WtMultinom\ percent of the weight, a real but minor share, the flexible
learners carrying the rest.  Whether that parametric form fits is thus settled
empirically rather than assumed.

The learned-proxy approach has well-documented pitfalls.
\citet{knoxlucascho2022} catalog these systematically:
measurement-stage uncertainty is routinely ignored, proxy quality is
asserted rather than tested, and the disconnect between prediction and
inference is papered over.  This paper follows their recommendations: out-of-fold validation
across the 25 imputation draws, measurement uncertainty propagated into
Stage~2 by a pairs-cluster bootstrap over all of them, calibration verified
by reliability diagrams.  The honest accounting is itself a result:
\DirMeasLo--\DirMeasHi\ percent of the coefficient variance originates in
the measurement stage, not in primary-analysis sampling---the measurement
model, not the regression, is the binding source of uncertainty, precisely
the situation \citet{knoxlucascho2022} warn about.

The paper proceeds as follows.  Section~\ref{sec:motivation} traces three
common accounts of bargaining breakdown through a single framework and
develops the expectations they stake on the shadow's two axes.
Section~\ref{sec:constructing} constructs and validates the shadow measure.
Section~\ref{sec:decision} turns to onset: it establishes that the shadow
predicts out of sample before weighing the mechanisms against one another, then
asks which interveners carry the effect.
