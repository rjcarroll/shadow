\section{A Learned Proxy for the Shadow of Intervention}
\label{sec:constructing}

Operationalizing the shadow requires a measure of the expected intervention
environment at the moment a potential rebel group weighs the costs and benefits
of open conflict.  The construction proceeds in two stages: a directed-dyad
classifier estimates intervention probabilities for every potential
intervener, and a country-year aggregation sums them under a Nash
fixed-point condition ensuring self-consistency.

\subsection{Outcome Variable}

The Stage~1 training labels draw primarily from \citet{regan2000},
whose dataset records third-party interventions in civil conflicts from 1944
to 1999.  I extend the coding through 2014 by hand-coding unambiguous
post-1999 military interventions---personnel-based only: troops, combat
advisors, or directed proxy forces; arms, aid, sanctuary, and UN peacekeeping
are insufficient---adding \PostExtInterventions{} directed dyad-year records
across \PostExtHosts{} hosts, for a combined table of
\CombinedInterventions{} records (1944--2014; coding and sources in Online
Appendix~\ref{app:coding}).

The civil war universe is drawn from the Correlates of War Intra-State War
Dataset (v5.1) \citep{dixon2016}, restricting to wars fought for central control
(type~4) or local/secessionist issues (type~5).  The COW threshold
($\geq$1,000 battle deaths) is theoretically appropriate: the mechanism requires
rebels organized enough to form rational expectations and conflicts large
enough that outside military involvement is a realistic prospect.\footnote{%
  The UCDP/PRIO Armed Conflict Dataset offers broader, more recent coverage and a
  more detailed external-support record; extending the learned-proxy approach to
  it is a natural next step (sketched in the conclusion), though its lower
  ($\geq$25-death) threshold---admitting many smaller conflicts in which
  intervention is rarer---would leave a sparser training signal.}
This yields \OnsetCYsColdWar{} distinct onset country-years between 1946 and 1999 and \OnsetCYsTotal{}
through 2014.  I restrict attention to \textit{military} interventions, coded as
\textit{government-biased}~(1) or \textit{opposition-biased}~(2).

For each directed dyad $(B \rightarrow A)$ active in a civil war onset year, the
training label is: 0 (no military intervention), 1 (government-biased), or 2
(opposition-biased).  Non-onset dyad-years are excluded from the training set.
This produces \OnsetCoded{} intervention-coded onset observations---\OnsetCodedGov{} government-biased
and \OnsetCodedOpp{} opposition-biased---across \OnsetHosts{} host countries and \OnsetInterveners{} unique intervening
states.  The five permanent Security Council members
account for \PFiveEvents{} of these events (\PFivePct~percent); the remaining
states---Cold War proxies, neighbors, regional patrons, and coethnics---account
for the majority (Table~\ref{tab:interveners}).

Training exclusively on onset country-years is not a convenience---it is
the only option.  Intervention in a civil war is undefined absent a civil war;
in non-onset years the outcome is not ``zero intervention'' but rather
unobserved.  The consequence is that the training sample is selected on
precisely the outcome that the Stage~2 analysis tries to explain: if the
shadow deters onset, then high-shadow country-years are systematically
underrepresented among onsets, and the classifier learns the
feature--intervention mapping from an endogenously filtered sample.  There
is no design that avoids this.  The defense rests on what the classifier
is asked to learn.  The training target is the conditional relationship
between \textit{dyad-level} features---alliance portfolios, capability ratios,
colonial ties, geographic proximity, regime similarity---and the direction
of military intervention, given that a war has started.  These bilateral
characteristics are plausible determinants of how state $B$ behaves toward
state $A$ in a conflict regardless of what brought that conflict about.
The fixed-point iteration then
handles the extrapolation: it asks what the equilibrium intervention
environment \textit{would} look like in any country-year, including those where
onset was never observed, without requiring that the training sample be
representative of the full population.

\subsection{Feature Set}

The classifier draws on \FeatXW{} directed-dyad-year features in four
families: characteristics of both the potential intervener and the
host---capabilities, regime, power status, and host-side conflict history and
social structure; bilateral ties---alliances, trade, proximity, colonial
history, disputes and their expected outcomes; foreign-policy
alignment---ideal-point distances and shared IGO memberships; and \FeatW{}
spatial lags capturing the weighted behavior of the other potential
interveners in the same host-year (Online Appendix~\ref{app:spatial}).  Every
time-varying feature enters at its value in the year preceding the onset, on
both sides of the dyad: the classifier's information set closes before the
onset year begins.  Many
variables enter twice---once for the intervener, once for the host---so the
classifier can learn asymmetric effects; every variable, with sources and
definitions, appears in Online Appendix~\ref{app:varlist}.  Missing data are
multiply imputed (five country-year $\times$ five undirected-dyad
imputations, yielding 25 complete datasets; Online
Appendix~\ref{app:imputation}), and the features are standardized and
reduced by principal components to 90\% of cumulative variance
(\PCAxwLo--\PCAxwHi{} components across draws).

\subsection{Classifier Design}

The multinomial outcome (0 = no intervention, 1 = government-biased,
2 = opposition-biased) is modeled using a super-learner ensemble
\citep{vanderlaan2007}.

Nine component classifiers spanning three model families are trained
within a ten-fold cross-validation scheme: two tree ensembles (a random
forest and two histogram-based gradient boosting
specifications at different learning rates), four logistic regression variants (ridge,
elastic-net, lasso, and unpenalized multinomial), and two multi-layer
perceptrons (shallow and deeper architectures).  The unpenalized
multinomial logit is the softmax best response of structural intervention
games \citep{gibilisco2022}, so the parametric benchmark competes inside the
library on out-of-fold performance.  Each classifier produces a vector of
class probabilities for every training observation.  The super learner combines these using
non-negative least squares (NNLS) weights chosen to minimize held-out
multinomial log-loss, so that the ensemble adaptively up-weights
component classifiers that predict well on out-of-fold observations.

All reported performance statistics are based on out-of-fold
predictions to guard against overfitting---following \citeauthor{wang2018}'s
(\citeyear{wang2018}) demonstration that
evaluation of class-imbalanced models on training data
produces severely inflated performance estimates \citep{muchlinski2016}.
Because intervention is rare, the metric matters: the primary summary is the
proportional reduction in log-loss against the class-frequency null (PRL), a
proper scoring rule; ROC-AUC is reported for comparability but is insensitive
to class imbalance, so the area under the precision--recall curve
(AUC-PR)---whose no-skill baseline is the prevalence itself---accompanies
it.

\paragraph{Spatial lags and Nash fixed-point iteration.}
The broader motivation for
the two-stage design is that intervention decisions are endogenous to the
conflict environment: interveners select into conflicts strategically, and
a single-equation model that treats realized intervention as a covariate in
an onset regression conflates the causal effect with the selection process
that generated it \citep{signorino2002,gent2008}; rebel groups in Bosnia and
Kosovo conditioned onset on anticipated support, so realized intervention is
partly a consequence of expectations rather than an independent cause
\citep{kuperman2008}.  Predicted expectations, not realized intervention, are
the appropriate regressor.

The spatial context variables of Appendix~\ref{app:spatial} depend on the
intervention choices of \textit{other} potential interveners---quantities
that are themselves to be predicted, and consequentially so: when one state
backs a government, rival states face a selective incentive to back the
opposition \citep{salehyan2011,findleyteo2006}, and it is the alignment
among interveners, not merely their count, that carries the signal
\citep{aydinregan2011}.  Without an explicit consistency requirement,
spatial lags computed from \textit{observed} training-data choices embed a
deterministic discrepancy between lag inputs and probability outputs
whenever the model is applied out of sample---systematic error, not sampling
noise, in a measure built to capture counterfactual environments.

The fix is to require that the predictions be self-consistent inputs to
themselves:

\begin{equation}
\sigma^*(B, A, t) = \hat{M}(X(B, A, t, \sigma^*)) \quad \forall\, (B, A, t),
\label{eq:fp}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\hat{M}$ is the trained super-learner classifier.  At $\sigma^*$,
the spatial lags are derived from the model's own equilibrium predictions,
not from historical observations.
The deterministic discrepancy disappears by construction.

This self-consistency condition is also the Nash equilibrium condition for
a natural class of games.  The primitive is the best-response
correspondence, not the game: a game specifies one \textit{derivatively},
via utility maximization, while $\hat{M}$ estimates one \textit{directly},
from the data---and Nash equilibrium is a fixed point of that
correspondence, whichever payoffs generate it.  $\sigma^*$ is thus the
equilibrium of every game whose best-response correspondence $\hat{M}$
estimates.\footnote{%
  The set of rationalizing payoff functions for any interior $\sigma^*$
  is non-empty---choose utilities that make each player indifferent among
  actions assigned positive probability.  What is identified is the
  correspondence; the payoffs that generate it are not.  A natural
  parametric fiber of these classes is the linear-utility family
  $u_i(a, \sigma_{-i}) = \alpha \cdot f_i(a) + \beta \cdot W_i(a, \sigma_{-i})$,
  where $f_i$ captures state-specific intervention incentives and $W_i$
  the spatial interaction term.  The best-response from this family is a
  softmax---the functional form of the multinomial logit component of
  the super-learner---so the parametric case is explicitly represented
  in the ensemble while the broader non-parametric mixture accommodates
  departures from it.  The iterative procedure that solves Equation~\eqref{eq:fp}
  is the nonparametric analogue of the nested pseudo-likelihood (NPL) algorithm
  of \citet{aguirregabiriamira2007}: the fitted
  super-learner replaces the parametric best-response function,
  and the fixed-point iteration updates beliefs (spatial lags) until
  self-consistency, as in A\&M's two-step PML.  In the parametric case,
  \citet{crismancox2021} show that this class of
  iterative estimators substantially outperforms direct maximum likelihood
  estimation of strategic games under equilibrium multiplicity.
  Uniqueness of the fixed point is not guaranteed in general; empirically the prediction-stage iteration
  converges for \FPconv{} of the 25 imputation draws within tolerance (mean
  \FPiterMean{} passes), the remainder plateauing just above it (Appendix~\ref{app:fp}).}
If the training data reflect equilibrium play and the lags are sufficient
statistics for the strategically relevant information in $\sigma_{-i}$, then
$\sigma^*$ estimates the fixed point of the true best-response
correspondence: Equation~\eqref{eq:fp} corrects a concrete measurement error
and identifies the equilibrium of the underlying intervention game at the
same time.

Equation~\eqref{eq:fp} has no closed form; it is solved by fixed-point
iteration in two stages: five warm-start passes during training, then, with
the classifier held fixed, prediction-stage updates of the spatial lags to
convergence---for every directed dyad-year, onset and non-onset alike, so
the shadow reflects the counterfactual equilibrium in any country-year
(Appendix~\ref{app:fp}).

The ensemble achieves a PRL of \SLprl\% and a macro-average ROC-AUC of
\SLrocauc.  For any intervention, the area under the precision--recall curve
reaches \SLaucprAny{}: averaged over the recall range, roughly a third of the
dyads the classifier ranks as likely interveners are genuine interventions,
versus fewer than one in a hundred (\AucprBase{}) at the no-skill baseline.
A PRL of \SLprl\% means the classifier recovers nearly two-fifths of the
information the class-frequency null discards---substantial for a
three-class problem whose modal outcome exceeds 99\% of observations.  The
natural worry is that it earns this by calibrating the hay---sharpening the
abundant non-intervention predictions---rather than finding the needles the
shadow requires.  Decomposing the log-loss reduction by true class rules
that out: \PRLfromEvents\ percent of the PRL originates in the
\DyadIntPct\ percent of observations where intervention actually occurred,
only \PRLfromCalib\ percent in improved calibration of non-events.

Because the classifier sees only publicly observable characteristics,
\SLprl\% is a lower bound on how foreseeable the intervention environment is
to the rebels themselves, who hold private intelligence and patron
communications besides---the empirical footing of the rational-expectations
reading (Appendix~\ref{app:model}, \emph{Remark: rational
expectations}).

The gap between the ensemble and the best single logistic
specification---\SLprl\% versus \BestLogitPRL\% PRL on an identical feature
set---reveals nonlinear structure that curated political-science feature
sets typically lack \citep{moruccispirling2024}; a measurement-grade feature
space is the exception that rewards flexible learners.  The NNLS weights say
so directly: ensemble mass concentrates on the nonlinear learners---the random
forest (\WtRF\%), the perceptrons (\WtMLP\%), and gradient boosting
(\WtHGB\%)---while the multinomial logit takes \WtMultinom\% and the penalized
logistic variants, despite the highest standalone PRL, receive near-zero
weight (Appendix~\ref{app:sl}, Table~\ref{tab:sl-components}).  With the
logistic family competing inside the library and down-weighted by the data,
the ensemble's advantage is not an artifact of architecture choice.

Because Stage~1 probabilities are subsequently summed across approximately
\NumInterveners{} potential interveners, systematic miscalibration compounds; reliability
diagrams confirming calibration for each outcome class appear in Appendix~\ref{app:sl}.
These diagnostics follow \citeauthor{knoxlucascho2022}'s (\citeyear{knoxlucascho2022}) recommendation to ``always
assess and report measurement performance'' (their \S5.4): the PRL and AUC
quantify predictive accuracy, and the reliability diagrams verify the
calibration assumptions on which the aggregation to country-year
expectations depends.

\subsection{Aggregating to Country-Year Intervention Expectations}

The Stage~1 classifier produces, for each directed dyad $(B \rightarrow A)$
in a civil war onset country-year, probability estimates
$\hat{\sigma}_B(1)$ and $\hat{\sigma}_B(2)$ for government- and
opposition-biased intervention, respectively.  The country-year
intervention expectation for host $A$ in year $t$ sums these across the
full roster of potential interveners, one total for each side:

\begin{equation*}
E_A^G(t) = \sum_{B} \hat{\sigma}_B(1), \quad
E_A^O(t) = \sum_{B} \hat{\sigma}_B(2).
\end{equation*}

Both sums are mechanically larger in later years as the system expands.
I include a year trend in all second-stage regressions to absorb this
secular growth.  The sums are also right-skewed; following
\citet{burbidge1988}, I apply the inverse hyperbolic sine (asinh)
transformation before entering them into the onset models.

\subsection{Properties of the Shadow Measure}
\label{sec:shadow-properties}

The measure behaves as the theory requires; Online
Appendix~\ref{app:properties} documents it: the
highest-probability dyads recover the historically correct patrons on the
correct sides in Angola, Ethiopia, and Afghanistan; the top-ranked
opposition-biased predictions are dominated by non-P5 interveners that a
five-power framework would miss; the distributions are heavily right-skewed;
and $E^G$ correlates moderately with the Lake hierarchy ($r = \CorrLake$)
and the \citet{gibilisco2022} structural estimates ($r = \CorrGM$).
