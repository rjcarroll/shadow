\section{Intervention and the Onset Decision}
\label{sec:motivation}

\subsection{The Shadow of Intervention}

A rebellion is launched in anticipation of how the world will answer it.
Before the first shot, a would-be opposition weighs not only the government in
front of it but the outside powers that might join the fight---some on its own
behalf, some on the incumbent's.  This is the shadow of intervention
\citep{cetinyan2002}, and two of its effects are by now well established.
Anticipated \emph{government}-biased intervention deters: a challenger that
expects outside backing to make the incumbent all but impossible to defeat
sees little gain in fighting, and does not rebel \citep{cunningham2016}.
Anticipated \emph{opposition}-biased intervention runs the other way, lowering
the expected cost of rebellion and raising its odds of success, and so
emboldens the challenge \citep{kuperman2008, thyne2006, qiu2022}.

Why should anticipation touch the decision to fight at all, rather than merely
the terms of a settlement?  Because a rebellion is not a unilateral leap: a
government that sees a credible challenge coming can buy it off with
concessions, so onset is better read as the breakdown of a bargain than as an
act taken in isolation \citep{walter2009}.  Were that bargain always available,
the shadow would move the division of the stakes without changing whether war
occurs.  That it moves onset is the trace of a friction in the bargain, and
rather than pick one in advance, this paper traces three common candidates
through a single bargaining framework:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Private information.}  The government's strength or resolve is
    its own to know, and the opposition's demand screens it---the classic
    risk--return account of war \citep{fearon1995}.
  \item \emph{Limited commitment.}  A government cannot credibly promise to
    honor tomorrow the concession that today's external threat would wring
    from it, so a peace that would avert war today is not believed to hold
    \citep{walter2009, powell2006}.
  \item \emph{Divergent expectations.}  The two sides read the shadow itself
    differently: the government and the rebels need not hold the same
    estimate of the odds of intervention, and a side that expects help grows
    optimistic and refuses a settlement it ought to accept
    \citep{cunningham2016, cetinyan2002}.
\end{itemize}
The three make overlapping but distinct predictions---they converge where any
costly-war bargain must, and part exactly where the data can look---and the
analysis lets the estimates weigh among them (Section~\ref{sec:decision}).

\subsection{Tilt and Intensity}

Government- and opposition-biased intervention rarely move one at a time.  A
country that draws anticipated intervention of one kind tends to draw the
other: the features that make
outside powers likely to back a government---its strategic value, its
alignments, its neighborhood---make them likely to back its challengers too.
What distinguishes one anticipated environment from another is therefore less
the sheer amount of expected intervention than two things at once: which way it
leans, and how heavy it is---its \emph{tilt} toward the opposition and its
\emph{intensity}.

The tilt governs the contest.  A net lean toward the opposition raises the
rebels' prospects and emboldens them; a net lean toward the government does the
reverse---the deterrent and emboldening logics above are the two directions of
a single axis.

The intensity governs the burden.  Because the two sides move together, a
high-intensity environment is one in which a war would draw heavy involvement
on \emph{both} sides at once---a competitive, internationalized war.  Such wars
are the hard ones to end: each added outside stakeholder with its own
preferences narrows the range of settlements all parties would accept, and
balanced support locks the fighting into stalemate \citep{cunningham2006,
cunningham2010, balchlindsayjoyce2008}.  A challenger who foresees such an
environment foresees a long, grinding war, and that prospect deters.  Because
states in fact intervene where wars are hardest to begin with \citep{gent2008,
gilligansergenti2008}, it is the \emph{anticipated} environment, not the
realized one, that bears on the decision to fight---and anticipated competing
intervention is itself associated with longer wars
\citep{akcinarogluradziszewski2005}.\footnote{These studies estimate the
duration of wars that occurred; that a rebel \emph{anticipating} such a war is
deterred from starting one is the inference drawn here, not a finding they
report.}

The two forces enter the value of fighting with a built-in asymmetry.  On
the government side they agree: outside backing for the incumbent both
worsens the rebels' odds and heightens the burden of the war they would
fight.  On the opposition side they compete: outside backing improves the
rebels' odds but heightens the burden of the very war in which the help would
arrive.  Whatever the bargain does with these values, then, government-biased
expectations should deter, while the opposition channel is a race the war
value alone does not resolve---a question the theory hands to the data.

The bargain is what turns war values into onset, and it is where the three
mechanisms part company.  All three expect onset to fall with the intensity:
in a bargain over a costly war, a heavier war leaves more for peace to save,
whichever friction is at work.  The tilt divides them.  Under the screen as
written---whatever the government's private object---the balance of power
passes into the demand and out of the decision to fight; no positive tilt
effect emerges.  Under limited commitment or divergent readings of the
shadow, onset rises with the tilt.  A positive tilt effect in the data would
therefore do more than sit comfortably with bargaining logic; it would point
away from the screen---which as written could not have produced it---and toward
the other two.

the comparison.

The logic is deliberately spare, and one game form carries all three
mechanisms.  Reduced to its frame, the decision is a would-be rebel weighing
whether to challenge in anticipation of how the outside world---every state
that might back the government, back the opposition, or stand aside---would
answer the war that follows (Figure~\ref{fig:ntree}).  The mechanisms differ
only in what the bargain must survive: private strength or resolve, promises
that cannot bind, readings of the shadow that do not agree.  The tilt and intensity of
the environment are summaries of the anticipated answer, aggregated across
everyone who might act; the appendix works the game's payoffs and equilibria
under each friction, and here its structure is enough.

That structure shows what a study of onset must measure.  The value of fighting
is built from two ingredients---who is expected to intervene, and how much each
intervention would matter.  Under two simplifying assumptions---that
interveners' contributions enter additively, and that a state expected to stand
aside adds nothing---it is a weighted sum over the full roster of potential
interveners,
\begin{equation}
U_O(F; \sigma) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[\sigma_i(O)\, u_O^i(O) + \sigma_i(G)\, u_O^i(G)\right],
\label{eq:EU}
\end{equation}
one term for each of the two unknowns.  The intervention probabilities
$\sigma_i$ are a prediction problem---for every potential intervener, the
probability and direction of its involvement---and the work of
Section~\ref{sec:constructing}, where flexible, data-driven methods earn their
place.  The utility weights $u_O^i$ are recoverable only from the onset
behavior the expectations are meant to explain, and are estimated in
Section~\ref{sec:decision}.  The measure is built in the first stage; its
bearing on onset---and with it, the standing of the mechanisms---is read in
the second.
