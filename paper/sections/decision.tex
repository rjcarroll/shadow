\section{The Decision to Fight}
\label{sec:decision}

Measure in hand, I study whether intervention expectations affect the
decision to initiate civil war.  The \textit{Entrants} model augments a standard
onset logit with the two shadow variables constructed in the previous
section:

\begin{equation*}
U_O(F; \hat{\sigma}) = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \gamma^O \sum_i \hat{\sigma}_i(O) + \gamma^G \sum_i \hat{\sigma}_i(G) + \epsilon,
\end{equation*}

\noindent where $\mathbf{X}$ contains the baseline country-year predictors and the two
summations are the expected total number of opposition-biased and
government-biased interveners, respectively.\footnote{In estimation the summations enter through the inverse
hyperbolic sine transform for variance stabilization---a concavity the
theory neither implies nor rules out (its content is the pair of axis
signs, Section~\ref{sec:motivation}).  Because asinh is strictly
increasing, coefficient signs are invariant to the choice, and the
out-of-sample comparisons do not turn on it; the exact shape of the
utility function is not identified from these data.}
This is the simplest
specification of Equation~\eqref{eq:EU}, in which all potential interveners carry the same
utility weight ($u_O^i(O) = \gamma^O$ and $u_O^i(G) = \gamma^G$ for
all $i$); the heterogeneous-utilities extensions below relax it.

The two-stage design extends the logic of statistical backwards induction
\citep{bas2008} to an $n$-player environment: the intervention subgame
involves up to \MaxInterveners{} simultaneous movers, separability and the Nash fixed-point
reduce it to one in which each intervener's marginal probabilities suffice,
and Stage~2 recovers the opposition's utility parameters from their
aggregate effect on onset.

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}

Whether intervention expectations shape onset is, in the first instance, a
question of model comparison.  Following \citet{clarke2007}, the relevant test
is not whether the shadow variables are individually distinguishable from
zero---for a generated and internally collinear regressor, the conventional
apparatus is ill-suited to that question\footnote{The two channels are nearly
the same variable in these data: $E^G$ and $E^O$ correlate at $r \approx
\CorrEgEo$, because a country that draws one kind of anticipated intervention tends
to draw the other.  An individual coefficient asks how onset moves with one
channel while the other is held fixed---a comparison the data almost never
supply---so individual standard errors are large and unstable whatever the
estimator.  Section~\ref{sec:direction} works instead in the two combinations
the data do identify: the sum of the channels and their difference.}---but
whether a model that includes them predicts better, out of sample, than one
that does not.  The worth of a
variable is then measured by what its removal costs: I compare the
out-of-sample fit of the onset model with and without the
shadow---leave-one-covariate-out importance \citep{lei2018}, the reduced-model
logic that \citet{mcalexandermentch2020} bring to onset
prediction---applied here across the whole covariate set.  A measure that improves on a
purely structural forecast---even modestly, since \emph{out-of-sample} fit is
the one signature overfitting cannot counterfeit---earns a reading of the
direction in which it enters.  This is a weaker standard than significance, and a more honest one
for a learned proxy: it asks the shadow to carry information a held-out
forecast can use, not to clear a threshold its generated and collinear
standard errors were never built to certify.

The rival is the \textit{Baseline}: a logistic onset model on the structural
covariates of \citet{fearon2003}---prior war, lagged log income and population,
mountainous terrain, oil, new-state status, political instability, ethnic and
religious fractionalization, and democracy---the specification the onset
literature has long treated as its reference.  This is not a replication of
Fearon and Laitin but their covariates estimated on the present sample (COW
Intra-State Wars v5.1, 1946--2014) with current sources: income and population
from V-Dem v15 \citep{vdem2025}, democracy from Polity, conflict history from
COW, and the fixed structural characteristics from the Fearon--Laitin
replication data.  Time-varying covariates are lagged one year against
post-onset contamination, and a year trend absorbs the mechanical growth of the
summation-based shadow variables as the state system expands.  The
\textit{Entrants} model adds the two shadow variables to this Baseline;
everything that follows asks what they add.

Three questions organize what follows.  First, how much does the shadow
improve out-of-sample prediction, and how does its contribution compare with
the structural predictors alongside it?  I assess this by leave-one-country-out
cross-validation---all of a country's observations held out
together.  Because onset is rare, I
report the proportional reduction in log-loss and the area under the
precision--recall curve, each against its no-information baseline, under both a
logistic specification and a random forest, so the comparison does not turn on
functional form.  Second, in what direction does the measure enter?  The
mechanisms of Section~\ref{sec:motivation} stake different predictions on the
two axes the data can identify, and I read the sign structure in the register
\citet{gill1999} and \citet{ward2010} urge for measurement-based
claims---direction and out-of-sample behavior in place of the asterisk.  Third,
which interveners carry the effect---does resolving the shadow by intervener
type strengthen the prediction and recover structure the single summed
expectation conceals?

Two features of the design demand that uncertainty be handled with care.  The
shadow is estimated at Stage~1, not observed, so treating it as data would
understate Stage~2 uncertainty; and its two channels are collinear, more than
the individual coefficients can be asked to separate.  I retain all 25
multiply-imputed shadow measures and propagate their spread by the $T \times P$
procedure of \citet{knoxlucascho2022}: the analysis runs on each of the $T = 25$
measurement draws, bootstraps over resampled countries within each, and pools
the resulting coefficient vectors.  Point estimates of fit average the held-out
predictions across draws; every interval reflects the full measurement spread.  This is
the discipline a learned proxy demands: its reach must be carried into
inference honestly, uncertainty and all.

\subsection{Prediction and Subsumption}
\label{sec:prediction}

Begin with the shadow's predictive worth.
Across the onset model's full covariate set, no variable's absence degrades
out-of-sample fit more than the shadow's (Table~\ref{tab:dropcol}): removing
both shadow variables lowers the out-of-sample proportional reduction in
log-loss further than removing any structural predictor, prior war included,
and the government channel alone outranks every canonical covariate.  In a
rare-event onset model most structural predictors buy little out of sample;
against that backdrop the shadow buys the most.  The
\citet{mentchhooker2016} prediction-difference test agrees jointly at every
configuration, its permutation counterpart in every measurement draw
(Appendix~\ref{app:pstests}).

The levels agree.  Onset is rare---\OosOnsets\ onsets in \OosN\
country-years, a no-skill precision--recall baseline of \OosPrevFloor---and
adding the two aggregate shadow variables to the structural \textit{Baseline}
raises out-of-sample AUC-PR from \OosBaseAucpr\ to \OosEntAucpr\ under
leave-one-country-out cross-validation, a paired improvement whose bootstrap
interval excludes parity (Table~\ref{tab:oos-gate}).  The aggregate gain is
modest; it grows to more than double the baseline once the shadow is resolved
by intervener type (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}).  It holds under both a
logit and a random forest.

Neither of the field's existing proxies for anticipated intervention was
built to predict onset: Cunningham's serves a hypothesis test about hierarchy
and deterrence, and the payoff of the Gibilisco--Montero structural estimates
is counterfactual analysis, which a predictive measure does not supply.  The
question Table~\ref{tab:subsumption} asks is narrower---for the onset
problem, does the shadow hold the information they hold?  On each proxy's
coverage subsample, adding the Cunningham security-hierarchy measure or the
Gibilisco--Montero P5 estimates to the shadow leaves out-of-sample fit
unchanged, while adding the shadow to either improves it
substantially---a gap that widens sharply once the shadow is resolved by
type (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}).

\subsection{Direction}
\label{sec:direction}

I turn to the direction in which the shadow enters.  The three mechanisms of
Section~\ref{sec:motivation} enter with overlapping bets: all three expect
onset to fall with the common intensity of the anticipated environment;
commitment and divergent expectations expect it to rise with the net tilt
toward the opposition, where the common-prior screen does not; and all agree
that government-biased expectations deter, while the raw opposition channel is
left unsigned---a race between the better odds outside backing brings and the
heavier war it portends.  The raw estimates behave accordingly:
government-biased expectations enter negative in \DirGovFrac\ percent of the 25
measurement draws, the opposition channel positive in \DirOppFrac\ percent and
fragile (Table~\ref{tab:direction}).  Their individual magnitudes cannot be
trusted---with the two channels nearly collinear ($r \approx \CorrEgEo$), the
individual coefficients swing from one measurement draw to the next.

What the data can speak to is not either channel alone but the two directions
in which the pair moves: \emph{together}---deeper into the shadow of both kinds
of intervention at once---and \emph{apart}---the balance tilting toward one
side.  These are the sum and the difference of the channels, a \textit{common
intensity} $E^O + E^G$ and a \textit{net tilt} $E^O - E^G$, and they are
precisely the coordinates in which Section~\ref{sec:motivation} stated its
predictions: the intensity that moves the burden, the tilt that moves the
contest.  Both are well identified where the individual channels are not, and
the two combinations the data can estimate are exactly the two on which the
mechanisms disagree.

Read in these terms, the intensity effect is unambiguous and the tilt effect
is real but softer.  The common intensity enters negative in \emph{every} one
of the 25 measurement draws, its pooled interval lying entirely below zero: a
heavier anticipated environment, of either kind, lowers the risk of onset.  The
net tilt enters positive in \DirTiltFrac\ percent of draws---a clear majority,
though its pooled interval still straddles zero (Table~\ref{tab:direction}).  I lean on the
signs rather than the magnitudes by necessity: between \DirMeasLo\ and
\DirMeasHi\ percent of each coefficient's variance is the shadow's own
measurement error, carried from Stage~1 and invisible to the conventional
standard error.  Both moves are large: for a country at the sample mean,
shifting the intensity from its tenth to its ninetieth percentile lowers
predicted onset from \DirCommonPlo\ to \DirCommonPhi\ percent
(Table~\ref{tab:direction}).

The pattern is suggestive about mechanism, though it does not decide.  The
intensity effect is common property of any costly-war bargain---it corroborates
the burden the theory attaches to intervention without selecting among the
frictions.  The tilt is the discriminating axis: a positive tilt cannot arise
through a frictionless bargain, and the common-prior screen as written does not
produce it either, whatever the government's private object
(Appendix~\ref{app:model}), so onset rising with the tilt sits more comfortably
with limited commitment or divergent readings of the shadow than with the
screen.  The evidence for the tilt is a clear-majority sign rather than a sharp
interval, so I read it as leaning against the screen, not ruling it
out.  The firmer trace of the opposition mechanism comes
not from the aggregate tilt but from resolving the shadow by intervener type,
to which I turn next.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Utilities}
\label{sec:heterogeneity}

The Entrants model weights every potential intervener equally.  The theory does
not require that: letting the opposition's valuation of an intervener vary with
the intervener's characteristics produces, on aggregation, a type-weighted
shadow term for each attribute.  I resolve the shadow along six intervener
attributes---major-power status (\textit{Powers}), contiguity to the host
(\textit{Neighbors}), a shared dominant ethnic group (\textit{Coethnics}), a
former colonial tie (\textit{Rulers}), bilateral rivalry (\textit{Rivals}), and
the local military balance---and ask what the type structure adds.

It adds a great deal, and the aggregate's modest predictive gain becomes a
large one.  Resolving the shadow by type raises out-of-sample AUC-PR to
\OosFullAucpr---\OosFullLift\ times the structural baseline, and nearly double
the aggregate shadow (Table~\ref{tab:oos-gate}).  The gain rewards structure,
not flexibility: the type-weighted logit out-predicts a random forest on the
same inputs (\OosFullAucprRF), so the theory's separate-weight-per-attribute
form carries the content, not black-box curvature.  The disaggregation lifts
both channels (Table~\ref{tab:channels}): resolving the government channel by
type raises its AUC-PR from \ChanGovAgg\ to \ChanGovFull, the opposition channel
from \ChanOppAgg\ to \ChanOppFull.  The opposition shadow, the weaker arm of
Section~\ref{sec:direction} in aggregate, gains the most, closing to
near-parity with the government side once the interveners behind it are held
apart.

Resolved this way, the shadow also dominates the existing proxies outright.  On
the Cunningham and Gibilisco--Montero coverage subsamples the full type-resolved
model reaches AUC-PR \SubFullLake\ and \SubFullGM, more than double the
baseline augmented with each proxy, which adds nothing to the full shadow
(Table~\ref{tab:subsumption}).  The proxies measure a corner of what the shadow
measures.

Which interveners, and in which direction?  Prediction gates the reading: I take
the direction of a type only where resolving the shadow by that type improves
prediction over the aggregate.  Three types clear that bar---neighbors
(\HetNeighborsOos), major powers (\HetPowersOos), and rivals
(\HetRivalsOos)---and for those three the per-type net tilt is stable across the
measurement draws (Table~\ref{tab:het-direction}).  Down the opposition column
the effect is not one thing: expected opposition-biased intervention by a
neighbor or a rival \emph{emboldens}, while by a major power it \emph{deters}
(Table~\ref{tab:het-direction}).  The government side, where it predicts, deters
for every type---the one per-type sign the theory fixes, no intervener
emboldening from the government's side, and no type contradicts it.

The opposition is emboldened, then, by the interveners with a proximate,
adversarial stake in the conflict---the neighbor and the rival, whose backing a
would-be rebel can read as committed\footnote{The rival channel has a natural
micro-foundation in \citet{qiu2022}, who shows that states sponsor insurgents in
their adversaries to drain them for leverage in interstate disputes---giving a
rival a standing interest in a rebellion's survival, and a would-be challenger a
backer it can count on.  What that model argues from cases, the shadow recovers
as an aggregate regularity across the full population of interveners.}---while
the prospect of great-power
sponsorship cuts the other way---in the model's terms, the signature of an
intervener whose anticipated involvement would internationalize the war more
than tilt it ($k' b_i > \pi' w_i$; Appendix~\ref{app:model}).  Why
great-power involvement carries that weight I leave open.  But the reversal
resolves the puzzle Section~\ref{sec:direction} left open: the
aggregate opposition shadow looked weak because it was summing effects of
opposite sign.  The emboldening was never absent from the data; it was averaged
away, resident in the composition of who is expected to intervene rather than in
a single summed expectation.
